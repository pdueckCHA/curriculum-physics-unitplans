Unit Overview

This 5-block unit bridges Newton's Laws and Energy. It covers Momentum, Impulse, and Conservation using Hewitt's Conceptual Physics Chapter 8.

Class Structure

This unit is taught to two separate class sections on an A/B block schedule (75-minute periods):

  • Honors Physics: 10 students (STEM-focused, stronger math background)
  • Regular Conceptual Physics: 15 students (mixed math readiness)

Each block shows differentiated content for both sections. The teacher delivers a full 75-minute lesson to each class separately.

The Big Idea (Hewitt)

"Momentum is conserved for all collisions as long as external forces don't interfere."

Teaching Context

Instructional framework and expectations for lesson delivery.

CHA Lesson Structure

Each lesson should include these components (cycle DI→Practice→Check→Adjust for each main concept):

  1. Lesson Objective: Clear, measurable learning target posted for students.
  2. Anticipatory Hook: Engaging opener connecting to prior knowledge or real-world context.
  3. Direct Instruction/Modeling: Teacher explains and demonstrates the concept.
  4. Guided Practice: Students practice with teacher support (whiteboard problems, think-pair-share).
  5. Checking for Understanding: Quick assessment of student grasp (thumbs, exit ticket, verbal check).
  6. Lesson Adjustment: Reteach, clarify, or extend based on check results.
  7. Independent Practice: Students work problems without direct support (primarily homework).
  8. Lesson Wrap Up: Summary, preview of next lesson, questions.

Big Ideas

Enduring understandings for the unit, linking momentum to safety and conservation laws.

  • Momentum is "Inertia in Motion"

    A quantity (p = mv) that dictates how hard it is to stop an object. Distinct from mass (inertia) and energy.

  • The Impulse-Safety Theorem

    Changing momentum requires Force over Time (Ft = Δmv). Extending impact time (airbags, bending knees) reduces injury-causing force.

  • Conservation is Absolute

    In a closed system (no external forces), momentum is never lost, only transferred. Total momentum before = total momentum after.

  • Bouncing Amplifies Force

    Objects that bounce undergo greater change in momentum (Δp = 2mv) than objects that stick, requiring larger impulse.

Standards

Power standards addressed, focusing on conservation and collision forces.

  • HS-PS2-2: Use mathematical representations to support the claim that the total momentum of a system of objects is conserved when there is no net force on the system.
  • HS-PS2-3: Apply scientific and engineering ideas to design, evaluate, and refine a device that minimizes the force on a macroscopic object during a collision.
  • Mathematical Practices: Algebraic rearrangement of Ft = Δmv, solving 1D inelastic collision equations, and (Honors) geometric application of the Pythagorean theorem for 2D vectors.

Key Vocabulary

Terms requiring explicit instruction. Students should be able to define, identify, and apply these concepts.

Momentum (8.1)

Inertia in motion; mass × velocity. Symbol: p. Units: kg·m/s

Formula: p = mv

Impulse (8.2)

Force × time interval; the cause of momentum change. Units: N·s (equivalent to kg·m/s)

Formula: J = Ft = Δp

Law of Conservation of Momentum (8.4)

In the absence of external forces, the total momentum of a system remains unchanged.

Formula: pbefore = pafter

Elastic Collision (8.5)

Collision where objects bounce without permanent deformation or heat generation. Kinetic energy is conserved.

Inelastic Collision (8.5)

Collision where objects become distorted and/or generate heat. In a "perfectly inelastic" collision, objects stick together.

Supporting Terms

These terms should be understood but may not require explicit instruction:

  • System: The collection of objects being analyzed
  • Internal forces: Forces between objects within the system
  • External forces: Forces from outside the system
  • Vector quantity: Has both magnitude and direction

Common Misconceptions

Predictable student errors to anticipate and address. These often stem from intuitive physics conflicting with Newtonian mechanics.

❌ "Impulse equals momentum"

Correction: Impulse equals change in momentum. An object can have large momentum but zero impulse if nothing is changing it.

Address in: Block 1, when introducing impulse-momentum theorem.

❌ "Heavier objects always have more momentum"

Correction: Momentum depends on both mass AND velocity. A fast car can have more momentum than a slow truck. A truck at rest has zero momentum.

Address in: Block 1. Use the truck vs. roller skate example from Hewitt.

❌ "Momentum is conserved only in elastic collisions"

Correction: Momentum is conserved in ALL collisions (elastic and inelastic) as long as no external forces act. What differs is whether kinetic energy is conserved.

Address in: Block 2/3. The freight car coupling problem shows conservation in an inelastic collision.

❌ "Force and impulse are the same thing"

Correction: Impulse = Force × Time. The same impulse can result from a large force over a short time OR a small force over a long time. This is why airbags work.

Address in: Block 1. Haystack vs. wall example.

❌ "The bigger object exerts more force in a collision"

Correction: Newton's 3rd Law—the forces are equal and opposite. What differs is the acceleration (and thus velocity change) due to different masses.

Address in: Block 2. Bug vs. windshield example (test question #50).

❌ "Forgetting that momentum is a vector"

Correction: Direction matters! Objects moving in opposite directions have opposite-signed momenta. This is critical for conservation problems.

Address in: Block 4. When setting up collision equations, establish a sign convention (+/−).

❌ "Confusing momentum with kinetic energy"

Correction: Momentum is mv (linear in velocity); kinetic energy is ½mv² (quadratic). They have different units and different conservation rules. Double your speed → double your momentum, but quadruple your kinetic energy.

Address in: Throughout, but especially when discussing elastic vs. inelastic collisions.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to...

All Students (Regular)

  • Define Momentum and calculate it using p = mv.
  • Apply the Impulse-Momentum theorem to explain safety features.
  • Differentiate between Elastic (bouncy) and Inelastic (sticky) collisions.
  • Calculate the final velocity of a 1D "perfectly inelastic" collision.
  • Explain Conservation of Momentum conceptually.

Honors Students (Additionally)

  • Resolve 2D momentum vectors using right-triangle geometry.
  • Derive the "stopping time" of a recoiling object using a multi-step Momentum → Impulse strategy.
  • Analyze vector components in "glancing blow" collisions.

Summative: Unit Test (Block 5)

Test Format (Regular)

  • True/False: 5 questions on core concepts
  • Multiple Choice: 8 questions (conceptual application)
  • Math Problems: 2 problems
    • Calculate momentum given mass and motion data
    • Conservation problem (e.g., roller skate push-off)
  • Essay: 1 problem—the "Freight Car Coupling" problem (diesel engine hits stationary flatcar, find final velocity)

Honors Addendum

Additional problems assessing:

  • 2D vector analysis (Traffic Accident or Billiard Ball problems)
  • Multi-step derivations (momentum → impulse → force/time)

Canonical Test Problems

These problem types appear on the test and should be practiced in class and homework:

  1. Runner momentum: Given mass (70 kg) and motion (400 m in 50 s), find average momentum. Requires finding velocity first.
  2. Roller skate conservation: Two people push off each other on frictionless skates. Given one's resulting velocity, find the other's.
  3. Freight car coupling: Engine (4× mass of flatcar) at 10 km/h hits stationary flatcar. They couple. Find final velocity. (This is THE canonical inelastic collision problem.)

Block 1

Topics: Momentum (8.1) & Impulse (8.2)

Learning Objective

Students will define momentum, calculate it using p = mv, and explain how impulse changes momentum.

Reading Quiz

Short quiz at block start on Hewitt 8.1-8.2.

Key Concepts to Teach

  • Momentum = mass × velocity (p = mv)
  • Impulse = force × time (J = Ft)
  • Impulse-momentum theorem: Ft = Δ(mv)
  • Extending time reduces force (for same impulse)

Canonical Examples

  • Truck vs. Roller Skate: Which has more momentum? (Depends on both mass AND velocity. Truck at rest has zero momentum.)
  • Haystack vs. Wall: Car crashes into each at same speed. Same momentum change, same impulse—but very different forces because of different times.
  • Catching a Ball: Why extend your hands backward? Increases time, reduces force.
  • Car Crumple Zones: Video analysis of slow-motion crash footage showing how crumpling extends impact time.

Regular Section

Focus on conceptual understanding via analogies. Emphasize the inverse relationship: Long time = Small Force. Practice basic p = mv calculations.

Honors Section

Derive Ft = Δmv from Newton's Second Law (F = ma, where a = Δv/t). Rearrange to show why the equation works.

Formative Assessment Ideas

  • Pre-Math Warmup: Can students find velocity from v = d/t? (Prerequisite for momentum calculations)
  • Whiteboard: Given mass and velocity, calculate momentum.
  • Think-Pair-Share: Why do gymnasts use thick mats?

Homework Alignment

Assign problems requiring: (1) basic p = mv calculations, (2) conceptual impulse questions (which surface is safer?), (3) reading check questions from Hewitt.

Block 2

Topics: Bouncing (8.3) & Conservation (8.4)

Learning Objective

Students will explain why bouncing increases impulse and state the law of conservation of momentum.

Reading Quiz

Short quiz at block start on Hewitt 8.3-8.4.

Key Concepts to Teach

  • Bouncing requires greater impulse than stopping (Δp = 2mv for complete reversal)
  • Internal vs. external forces
  • Defining a "system"
  • Conservation: pbefore = pafter (when no external forces)

Canonical Examples

  • Flower Pot on Head: Why is a bouncing pot worse than one that stops? (Greater Δp means greater impulse means greater force.)
  • Pelton Wheel: Curved paddles cause water to bounce (U-turn), doubling the impulse compared to flat paddles.
  • Cannon Recoil: System momentum starts at zero. Ball gains +p, cannon must gain −p. Momentum is transferred, not created.
  • Newton's Cradle: Demonstration of momentum transfer in elastic collisions.

Regular Section

Conceptual focus: "Why does the cannon kick back?" Answer: The cannon-ball system starts with 0 momentum. Ball gets +p, cannon must get −p.

Honors Section

Introduce variable-mass problems conceptually (rocket propulsion). The mass of the system changes as exhaust is expelled.

Formative Assessment Ideas

  • Whiteboard "System" Checks: Draw a circle around the system. Identify which forces are internal vs. external.
  • Prediction: If I fire a dart at a box, will the box move more if the dart sticks or bounces?

Homework Alignment

Assign problems on: (1) bouncing vs. stopping impulse comparisons, (2) identifying systems and internal/external forces, (3) conceptual conservation questions.

Block 3

Topic: The Momentum Lab

Learning Objective

Students will collect data to verify conservation of momentum and distinguish between elastic and inelastic collisions.

Hands-on block using collision carts or marbles. Goal: provide tangible experience with conservation before tackling collision algebra. Lab is hard-limited to one block—design must ensure completion within 75 minutes.

Lab Focus

  • Compare "Sticky" (inelastic) vs. "Bouncy" (elastic) collisions
  • Measure/calculate pbefore and pafter
  • Verify that momentum is approximately conserved
  • Account for discrepancies (friction as external force)

Regular Section

Focus on qualitative and basic quantitative analysis. "Does pbefore ≈ pafter?" Observe and describe the difference between sticky and bouncy collisions.

Honors Section

Lab includes mandatory Error Analysis section. Students must quantitatively estimate friction's effect and explain why momentum was not perfectly conserved.

Deliverable

Lab Report due (summative assessment). Should include data tables, calculations, and conclusion about conservation.

Block 4

Topics: 1D Collisions (8.5) / 2D Vectors (8.6)

Learning Objective

Regular: Solve 1D inelastic collision problems algebraically. Honors: Apply conservation in 2D using vector addition.

This is where Regular and Honors content diverges most sharply. Both sections apply concepts from Blocks 1-3 to mathematical problem-solving—making this block function as integrated review.

Reading Quiz

Short quiz at block start on assigned reading.

Regular Section: 1D Collision Drill

Goal: Complete mastery of algebra for 1D inelastic collisions.

The Equation:

m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = (m₁+m₂)vfinal

Canonical Problems:

  • Freight Car Coupling: 4m engine at 10 km/h hits stationary 1m flatcar. Find vfinal. (Answer: 8 km/h)
  • Big Fish/Little Fish: 6-kg fish at 1 m/s swallows 2-kg fish at rest. Find vfinal. (Answer: 0.75 m/s)
  • Roller Skate Push: 30-kg girl pushes 25-kg boy, boy moves at 1 m/s. Find girl's speed.

Activity: Whiteboard drill. Solve for vfinal, m₁, v₁, etc.

Note: No vectors. Focus purely on algebra mastery.

Honors Section: 2D Vector Extension

Goal: Apply conservation laws in two dimensions.

The Approach:

Momentum is conserved separately in x and y directions. Use Pythagorean theorem to find resultant.

Canonical Problems:

  • Traffic Accident: Car A going east, Car B going north, they collide and stick. Find direction and speed of wreckage.
  • Billiard Ball: Cue ball hits stationary ball at angle. Analyze resulting momentum vectors.
  • Exploding Firecracker: Falling firecracker explodes into two pieces. Vector sum of fragment momenta equals original.

Activity: Collaborative problem solving with vector diagrams.

Review Integration

Both tracks inherently review prior content: students must recall p = mv (Block 1), conservation principle (Block 2), and collision types (Block 3) to solve these problems.

Homework Alignment

Regular: 4-6 coupling/collision problems similar to test format. Honors: 2-3 additional 2D vector problems.
